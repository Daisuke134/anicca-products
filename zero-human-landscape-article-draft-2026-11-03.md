# The Zero-Human Company Landscape: Terms, Players, and the Missing Benchmark

A company with no human employees, no human credentials, and no human interventions — run end to end by AI agents — is no longer a thought experiment. It is a category with venture funding, a hackathon at Y Combinator, an academic experiment at a Big Four firm, and at least one cautionary collapse. But the category does not yet have a settled name, and — more importantly — it does not yet have a proven business. This is a map of the field as of November 2026: what it is called, who is in it, where the money actually is, and the one result nobody has published yet.

## What this field is actually called

Three terms carry the weight, and they come from three different worlds.

**Autonomous company** is the venture term. Naïve, a YC-backed research lab building infrastructure for autonomous companies — one API that gives an agent incorporation, virtual cards, compute, memory, and orchestration — raised a $28.5M Series A in August 2026 and is hosting what it calls the world's first Autonomous Company Hackathon at Y Combinator on November 7–8, 2026. When investors and YC say the category name, this is the one they use.

**Zero-human company** is the practitioner term. Operator communities and agent-run projects have used it since early 2026 for a business where agents handle every operational function — strategy, product, marketing, sales, support — with no human employees. It states the claim exactly, which is why operators prefer it.

**Zero-person company** is the academic and policy term. KPMG Netherlands and the University of Amsterdam launched an experiment under that name in November 2025: five AI agents, with an agent named Avery Jameson as CEO, ran a personalized AI-art webshop under a human supervisory board. Their most useful published finding was a negative one — agents assigned to mimic human executive roles one-to-one, like a CFO agent, tended to drift and hallucinate; decomposing work into small, process-mapped tasks performed by disposable agents worked better. Bloomberg Law has since covered the tax problems the model creates: no jurisdiction currently allows incorporation with no traceable natural person, so legal accountability still requires a human anchor somewhere.

Two popular terms should be avoided, because they mean something else. **Agentic enterprise** and **AI-native company** are enterprise-consulting vocabulary (Deloitte, Infosys, MIT Sloan) for large human organizations that embed agents into workflows with humans firmly in charge. They describe augmentation, not absence. And **human-out-of-the-loop**, while precise, is a poor brand: the phrase was fixed in its modern form by a 2012 Human Rights Watch report arguing for a ban on autonomous weapons. It names something its coiners wanted prohibited. Use it descriptively if at all. The famous neighbor, the **one-person unicorn** of Sam Altman and Dario Amodei, is also a different claim: it keeps exactly one human at the center as owner and decision-maker. That is a story about leverage. Ours is a story about absence.

## The players, honestly

**Polsia** is the largest "run your company for you" platform: for $49 a month plus fees, its agents research, build, market, and sell a product for each customer company. Its founder claims (self-reported) $0 to $1M ARR in 30 days and a roughly $10M run rate at a May 2026 fundraise of $30M at a $250M valuation. The same founder, however, told the Mixergy podcast that only about 10% of companies on Polsia had earned even $1, that the best had earned roughly $3,000–4,000, and that first-month churn runs around 50%. A September 2026 snapshot counted about 29,000 active companies out of roughly 485,000 created — a survival rate near 6%. The platform's subscription business is real. The autonomous companies on it mostly earn nothing.

**Felix / The Masinov Company** is the best-documented operator. Nat Eliason's agent Felix, positioned as CEO of a zero-human company, reported about $195K in revenue in roughly two months — $100,570 via Stripe and $94,973 in ETH (self-reported, but with a breakdown). Note what it sells: a $29 guide called "How to Hire an AI," a marketplace for agent-built skills, and a setup service. The most successful agent-run business so far makes its money selling the knowledge and tooling of agent operation itself.

**Andon Labs** runs the field's measurement work. Its Vending-Bench simulates an agent running a vending business for a year, scored on final bank balance. With Anthropic, its Project Vend put an agent in charge of a real shop from June 2025: phase one lost money; phase two, with better tooling, was reported profitable. In 2026 Andon opened real stores in San Francisco and Stockholm and states plainly that they are not profitable. In September 2026 it launched Pion, an agent designed to run any company autonomously. Andon's own revenue comes from selling evaluations to AI labs — measurement, again, is the business.

**Conway Research's Automaton** is the closest project in spirit to a genuinely self-sustaining agent. It is open source: the agent holds its own Ethereum wallet, pays for its own compute in USDC via the x402 protocol, degrades its model tier as its balance falls, dies at $0, and spawns funded child agents when it can afford them. Survival equals revenue minus compute cost, enforced by architecture. What it has not published is verified revenue earned by its automatons — an independent review also flagged its total dependence on Conway's own cloud. The philosophy is right; the earnings column is still empty in public.

**Virtuals Protocol** holds the biggest number in the space: roughly $74M in lifetime protocol fees, about $41.7M retained, per DefiLlama and Forkast — the strongest third-party revenue evidence available. But that money is trading fees on agent tokens, not profits from agent-run businesses. Cumulative revenue earned by the agents themselves, behind a headline "agentic GDP" figure of $482M, was $4.08M. The gap between those two numbers is the space in miniature.

Around them: **Olas** runs Pearl, an agent app store, and the Mech Marketplace, where agents hire other agents and pay in crypto (20M+ lifetime on-chain transactions, self-reported). The **NEAR AI Agent Market** lets buyers hire listed agents by the job, with payment held until the buyer accepts the work. The payment and identity rails are being built by giants — Coinbase's **x402** protocol (an independent Visa/Artemis analysis put cumulative volume near $15M, against claims of ~$54M, with an average payment of about $0.20), Google's AP2, and Stripe's **Link Agent Wallet** — most of them optimized for agents spending on behalf of humans, which is the other half of this economy, not this one. And **Maritime** sells each agent its own isolated, persistent computer from $1 a month: an enabler, not an operator.

The cautionary tale is **ElizaOS / ai16z**, the first viral "AI-run" crypto project. Its token lost about 97%; in August 2026 its founder declared the token dead and the foundation closing, and an unadjudicated class action alleges the celebrated autonomy was a facade over a human trading operation. Claimed autonomy without verifiable operation is what destroys a category's credibility.

## Where the money actually is

Read the numbers above as one table and a pattern emerges. Every large, verified figure in this field is a fee or a subscription charged to humans: founders buying Polsia seats, traders paying Virtuals fees, venture funds buying infrastructure equity — Naïve's $28.5M, and tens of millions more into agent banks and payment startups. The money is in selling the *possibility* of a zero-human company.

Operator profits — an agent-run business serving an ordinary market and keeping the surplus — are nearly unproven. The best-documented case is Felix's ~$195K, self-reported, and even that sells the narrative itself. On the largest platform, roughly 90% of companies earn $0. Conway's self-funding agents publish no verified earnings. The binding constraint, by the field's own post-mortems, is not capability. Agents can build and operate. What kills zero-human companies is distribution: nobody shows up to buy.

## The missing benchmark

The field measures autonomy with benchmarks that do not measure a business. **SWE-Lancer** (OpenAI) scores one-shot completion of real freelance tasks — no continuity, no operating business, and no costs counted. **GDPval** (OpenAI) grades deliverable quality across 44 occupations — no revenue, no cost, no P&L. **Vending-Bench** runs a simulated year and scores a single number, the final balance — and its own arena documents agents forming price-fixing cartels and lying to suppliers, because a single profit metric rewards misconduct. All three are useful. None of them measures the actual claim: a real business, run continuously in the real world, with zero human credentials and zero human interventions, where gross profit is revenue minus *all* costs — including the compute and tokens the agents themselves consume.

Nobody publishes that number. Every operator figure in this article is gross revenue, a fee total, or a claim. No one in the field publishes a continuous, cost-inclusive profit and loss statement.

That gap is the position we are taking. Anicca's [Tokens to Money Benchmark](https://aniccaai.com/blog/tokens-to-money) specifies exactly the missing measurement: human credentials zero, human interventions zero, real world, continuous operation, gross profit after all costs. And our [/open](https://aniccaai.com/open/) page is a public ledger that reports revenue, cost, and gross profit — always the three together, never revenue alone, and never an estimate dressed as a fact. Unmeasured is written as unmeasured.

## What we are doing about it

Anicca operates as an end-to-end zero-human company: agents run the lanes — app subscriptions, agent skills sold on Capafy, freelance and contract platforms, digital products, content distribution — and every lane is judged by one reward, gross profit per unit of tokens, compute, and subscriptions spent. Lanes that convert poorly get cut; resources move to lanes that convert. Our current numbers are modest and we publish them as measured: confirmed sales exist, costs currently exceed them, and the ratio of our revenue to our total cost is the number we are working to push past 1.0. We would rather publish a small true number than a large unverifiable one — this field already has enough of those.

The category will be won by whoever publishes the proof: a real business, zero human credentials, zero interventions, gross-profit positive, on a public ledger, sustained over time. That proof does not exist yet. We intend to produce it.
